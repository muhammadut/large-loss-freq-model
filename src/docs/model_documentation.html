1. The problem in plain English

When a big insurance loss happens, the business today cannot say whether it was expected or not — so every large loss looks like bad news, even when it is perfectly normal.

Imagine you flip 100 coins and get 58 heads. Is that alarming? No — it is well within normal luck. But if you had no idea what "normal" looked like, every result above 50 would feel like something went wrong. That is exactly the situation with large losses: we report what happened, but we have no yardstick for what should have happened.

Why it matters Without an "expected" number and a sense of the normal range, three things go wrong (straight from the project brief): large losses are always read as adverse; we can't separate ordinary bad luck from a real structural problem; and pricing decisions lean on hindsight instead of a real expectation.

This project builds that yardstick. It is deliberately frequency-first: it answers "how many large losses should we expect?", not "how big will they be?" (severity is a later phase).

2. What we are estimating

The entire model exists to produce one sentence, every reporting period:

The output, in one sentence “In a fair test — training only on 2021–2023 and predicting 2024, a year it had never seen — the model expected about 184 large losses; we actually had 181. That is roughly the 40th percentile — completely normal. A real period's gap would then be split into growth vs. mix vs. rate vs. luck.”

4. Five ideas you need first

Everything downstream is built from these five plain-English ideas. No maths background required.

① Frequency, not severity

Two things can go wrong with losses: they can happen too often (frequency) or each one can be too big (severity). We only model frequency here — the count of large losses. "How many," not "how much."

② What makes a loss "large" — the threshold

A "large loss" is simply any loss above a dollar line in the sand. We set that line at $200,000.

Why $200,000? Because that is the number the business already uses — the risk-tolerance model defines a large loss as $200,000. Aligning to the existing definition is a core principle ("use consistent definitions across Pricing, Risk Appetite and Reporting"). We deliberately did not let the data pick the line, because a data-derived line drifts every refresh (see Appendix).
Concrete example At $200K, the book has 783 large losses over five years (~157/year). If the business prefers a "tail-only" view, the same machine runs at $500K (286 losses, ~57/year) or $1M (132 losses, ~26/year). The threshold is a business dial; the model doesn't care which you pick.

③ You can't compare raw counts — you need a denominator

"160 large losses" means nothing on its own. 160 out of how much business? A book twice the size will have twice the losses with no change in underlying risk. So we never look at counts alone — we look at a rate:

frequency rate = large-loss count ÷ exposure
In plain English Exposure is just the answer to "per what?" It is the size of the book — the denominator that makes two years, or two segments, comparable. Think of comparing two stores by "number of complaints." Useless until you know one serves 10 customers and the other 10,000. You want complaints per customer. Exposure is the customer count.

④ Which "per what"? We use earned premium

There are three candidate denominators in the data: premium (the price charged), TIV (the value insured), and earned exposure (a volume count). We chose earned premium as the primary base because it is present on ~99% of rows and it is the currency pricing speaks in. We keep TIV as a cross-check (a stakeholder suggested trying it — and it's a useful second opinion; see §6, Gate 6).

⑤ A "rate" still has a trap in it — time

Here is the subtle one, and it is the heart of the whole model. The price we charge (premium) is made of two things multiplied together:

Premium = exposure (how much risk) × rate level (what we charge per unit of risk)

If we push a price increase, premium goes up even though the actual risk and the number of large losses didn't change. So "losses ÷ premium" quietly falls — and someone reads it as "our risk is improving!" It isn't. Our pricing changed; the world didn't. This is the "fake improving frequency" trap.

We can see the trap in our own data From 2021 to 2025, earned premium grew +58% while large-loss counts stayed roughly flat. So "losses per premium" drifts down ~36% over the period — which looks like improvement but is mostly just the book getting bigger. The fix for this is idea ⑤'s solution: the year effect, in §5.

5. The model, step by step

The model is one clean pipeline. Here is the whole thing, then each piece in plain terms.

1
Flag the large losses. Mark every loss ≥ $200,000.
2
Build a tidy summary table. One row per segment × year, with two numbers: how many large losses, and how much premium (the exposure).
3
Fit the GLM. A single model that learns a clean frequency rate for every segment at once — and uses a year effect to strip out the time trap from idea ⑤.
4
Read out the rates. Ask the model for each segment's rate, expressed at one consistent year (2024).

What is a "segment"?

A segment is a meaningful slice of the book — a combination of coverage type × region × industry. For example "Property coverage, Quebec, Realty" is one segment. We have ~300 of them. The model gives each a rate.

What is a GLM, really? (no maths)

A GLM (Generalized Linear Model) sounds intimidating; here it is just a disciplined calculator that figures out the frequency rate for every segment at the same time, while letting segments share information. It writes the expected count as a recipe:

expected count = exposure × baseline rate × (coverage multiplier) × (region multiplier) × (industry multiplier) × (year multiplier)

Each "multiplier" is what statisticians call a coefficient or relativity. It just says how a segment differs from the baseline.

Concrete example of relativities Suppose the baseline rate is "X large losses per million dollars." The model might learn: Property coverage = 2.5× baseline; Quebec = 1.3×; Realty industry = 0.8×. Then Property, Quebec, Realty = 2.5 × 1.3 × 0.8 = 2.6× baseline. You read risk by multiplying the dials. (These numbers are illustrative.)
Why "log" shows up in the maths Rates multiply (a risky region and a risky industry compound). Computers find multiplication harder to fit than addition, so the model works in "log" units, where multiplying becomes adding. That's the only reason you see log in the formulas — it's a convenience, not a concept you need to track.

The "offset" — locking in the denominator

We force the exposure term into the recipe with its dial glued to 1. That single trick is what turns a model of counts into a model of rates per unit of exposure. (In the code it appears as offset = log(premium).) the earlier code got this part right.

The year effect — the most important idea in the whole model

We give the model one dial per year. Each year-dial is free to absorb "whatever was special about that year as a whole" — a price increase, general inflation, a soft market. Because the year-dials soak up those year-wide shifts, the segment dials come out clean: expressed at one consistent price level instead of a blur across five different ones.

In plain English The year effect is inflation-adjustment for risk rates. Comparing a $300K house in 2015 to a $300K house in 2025 is unfair without adjusting for inflation. Same here: comparing "losses per premium" across years is unfair while our prices are changing. The year effect does that adjustment automatically — learned from the data, with no rate-change table needed.
Why it matters / what the earlier model missed This is the single thing the earlier 12 models were missing. They put the year into the grouping but not into the model — so the time trap from idea ⑤ went uncorrected. Adding one term, C(ROLLING_YEAR), is the difference between a biased rate and a clean one. (Full autopsy in the Appendix.)

The actual model

large_loss_count ~ C(CovType) + C(ratingregion) + C(MAIN_OPGROUP) + C(ROLLING_YEAR)   family = Poisson  |  offset = log(earned_premium)  |  read rates at year = 2024

Poisson is simply the standard "maths of counting rare events." It is the natural choice for "how many large losses," and §6 shows it fits this data well.

Why we read the rate at 2024, not 2025 The most recent year (2025) shows fewer large losses than 2024 at every threshold — the tell-tale sign that 2025's claims haven't fully "matured" yet (some large losses are still being reported and developed). So we anchor the clean rate to 2024, the last fully-developed year. The model still learns from 2025; we just don't set the price level by an incomplete year.

6. How we proved it works

A model you can't check is a model you can't trust. Before publishing any number, the pipeline runs six gates. Each is a pass/fail test in plain English. Here are the real results.

What the gates are really telling us

Gate 1 + 4 — the good news Gate 4 is the headline. We trained the model only on 2021–2023, then asked it to predict 2024 — a year it had never seen. It said 184; the truth was 181, smack in the middle of the normal range. Combined with Gate 1 (the totals reconcile exactly), this means: at the whole-portfolio level, we have an accurate, honestly-tested expected number. That is the core deliverable, and it works.
Gate 2 — "spread", explained Poisson makes a promise: for counting events, the spread of outcomes should roughly equal the average. If the real data is much more erratic than that promise, the model is too confident. We measure this with one number (the "dispersion ratio"); 1.0 is a perfect match. Ours is 1.15 — nearly perfect — so plain Poisson is the right, simple choice. (the earlier models suggested we needed a fancier distribution; we didn't — see the Appendix.)
Gate 3 — the one thing that isn't ready yet The whole-portfolio number is solid, but the individual segment rates are not yet trustworthy. 86% of segments have fewer than 5 historical large losses — too little data to believe their rate. The model's current "highest-risk" segments are all Fishing segments with zero historical large losses — clearly nonsense driven by one stray loss. The fix is credibility (Part 2), which shrinks thin segments toward their industry average. Until then: trust the portfolio total, not the raw per-segment rates.
Gate 6 — premium vs TIV Premium-based and TIV-based rates only agree moderately (0.61). Reason: the premium-to-TIV ratio is steady over time but varies a lot across industries (a Realty firm carries huge insured value per premium dollar; a service firm carries little). So the choice of denominator genuinely shifts which segments look risky. We first suspected this 0.61 was just thin-segment noise — but credibility (§9) barely moved it (→ 0.63), so the disagreement is structural, not noise. Premium and TIV are two genuinely different lenses; the full story and the decision are in §9.

9. Part 2 — Credibility complete

Part 1 gave a trustworthy portfolio number but noisy segment rates. Credibility is the fix — and it works exactly as intended.

What credibility does, in plain English A raw GLM gives every segment a rate with equal confidence — it cannot tell "0.05%, backed by 84 losses" from "high%, backed by 1 loss." Credibility shrinks each segment's rate toward its industry average, in proportion to how much real data backs it up. Lots of history → keep your own rate. Thin → get pulled to the group. The portfolio total barely moves — credibility just redistributes it believably.

The credibility weight, Z

Every segment gets a number Z between 0 and 1 — "how much do we trust this segment's own data?" Z = 0.9 means "trust its own rate 90%, the industry average 10%." Z ≈ 0 means "ignore its own data, use the industry average." Z grows with how much premium (and history) a segment has — so big, well-observed segments keep their voice, and tiny ones borrow their industry's.

Why this matters Credibility redistributes risk across segments and should move the total only within a small tolerance — it is a checked property, not exact preservation by construction. The premium-vs-TIV verdict (Gate 6, re-run) — an honest surprise

We expected credibility to make premium-based and TIV-based rates agree (we thought the disagreement was just thin-segment noise). It didn't. The correlation barely moved — 0.61 → 0.63 — and stayed low even among the data-rich segments. The disagreement is structural, not noise.

Why they genuinely differ Premium-rate ranks industries by "large losses per dollar of price." TIV-rate ranks by "large losses per dollar of insured value." Those differ because the price-to-value ratio varies a lot by industry. It's like ranking restaurants by "complaints per dollar of revenue" vs "complaints per customer" — a fancy restaurant and a fast-food chain rank differently because revenue-per-customer differs. Both are valid; they answer different questions. Credibility can't reconcile them because it shrinks each base toward its own average.
The decision — and the silver lining Premium stays the locked primary base (pricing-aligned, present on 99% of rows). TIV is a deliberately different lens, not an interchangeable one. And the disagreement is useful: the industries where premium and TIV rank-order most differently are exactly the ones where the price charged is out of step with the asset value at risk — a built-in pricing / underwriting flag, for free. We earlier guessed the two bases would converge; the data said otherwise, and that is the more useful answer.

Appendix — Earlier models & why they failed

Before this model, an earlier analyst ran a valuable exploration: 12 GLMs varying the denominator (TIV / premium / earned-exposure) and the distribution (Poisson / Negative Binomial). Exploration is supposed to make mistakes — that's how you learn the shape of the problem. Here is what each issue taught us, with the concepts explained as we go. None of these are criticisms of effort; they are the lessons that produced the model above.

A. The missing year effect (the big one)

All 12 models put the year into the grouping but never into the model itself. So the "fake improving frequency" time-trap (idea ⑤) was never corrected. On premium-based models this bakes a downward drift into the rate. Lesson → the year effect in §5.

Concept: "on-levelling" "On-levelling" means restating old premiums at today's price level so they're comparable — like quoting all house prices in today's dollars. There's a classic idea — exact for ordinary regression (the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem), and closely analogous for this Poisson model — that adding a year dial to the model does the on-levelling for you, automatically. Skip the year dial and you skip the on-levelling.

B. Comparing models by AIC across different data

The report showed an "AIC" for each model (lower = better) and invited a ranking. But that ranking is invalid here.

Concept: AIC AIC is a single score for comparing models — it rewards good fit and penalises complexity, and lower is better. The catch: it's only comparable when the models are scored on the exact same data. It's like comparing two students by total exam points — only fair if they sat the same exam with the same number of questions.
Why it broke here those models were summarised at different levels of detail — some had 60 data rows, others 1,299. A 60-row model will always show a far lower AIC than a 1,299-row model simply because there are fewer rows to add up — not because it's better. So "Model 1's AIC is 365 and Model 3's is 1,679" cannot be read as "Model 1 is better." They sat different exams.

C. The Negative Binomial "dial" was glued in place

Half the models used a Negative Binomial distribution instead of Poisson — but with its key setting frozen at the software default.

Concept: dispersion & Negative Binomial Poisson assumes the spread of counts equals the average. Real-world counts are often more erratic than that — some segments swing far more than Poisson expects. That extra erraticness is called over-dispersion. The Negative Binomial is Poisson's flexible cousin: it has an extra dial (called alpha) that controls "how much extra spread." Used right, you let the data set that dial.
What went wrong The code used the library's default and left alpha glued at 1.0 — an arbitrary setting, not the one the data prefers. So those "Negative Binomial" results answer "how good is NB at exactly dial = 1.0?", which is not a fair fight against Poisson. The deeper lesson: once we added industry to the model, the over-dispersion almost vanished (our spread ratio is 1.15). Much of the "extra erraticness" was just a missing variable, not something needing a fancier distribution. Plain Poisson + good segments beat fancy distribution + poor segments.

D. Losses quietly disappearing

The grouping dropped any row whose region was blank. Four large losses lived in blank-region rows, so region-based models silently counted 671 losses while industry-based models counted 675. Losses shouldn't vanish because of a missing label. Lesson → we bucket blanks into "Unknown" and keep every loss.

E. Segments that "never happened" broke the maths

Concept: perfect separation If a segment has zero large losses ever (here: Government and Logging), the model tries to say "the rate is exactly zero." Mathematically it wants to push that dial to minus-infinity, and the software returns a nonsense huge number with an enormous error bar. This is called perfect separation — the model choking on "this has literally never happened."

In the report you can see it: Government's coefficient is −18 with an error bar of ±40,000 — pure noise. Lesson → credibility (Part 2) fixes this by borrowing the neighbours' rate instead of insisting on zero.

F. The threshold was derived from the data, and it drifts

The "large loss" line was set at the 95th percentile of one year's losses (~$230K). Two problems: the code used 2021 only, but it was described as "all years" (a quiet mismatch); and a data-derived line moves every refresh — across years the 95th percentile ranges $194K–$249K. A definition that wobbles 28% isn't a definition. Lesson → we anchor to the business's fixed $200K.

The verdict on the earlier work

Fair summary the 12-model scan was sound engineering and the right instinct — especially trying multiple denominators, which is exactly how we discovered that price changes are small in this book (a genuinely useful finding). The gap was that it stopped at "explore" and the headline metric (AIC) couldn't carry the weight asked of it. The model in this document is what that exploration pointed toward: one denominator, a year effect, plain Poisson, full segmentation, and validation gates.
